251 The Project Presentation
1.1 Executive Summary
Qabs closes the gap between a fire starting and the people nearby knowing about it. A 30-node
pilot costs about 18,060 TND and pays for itself if it keeps about 7 ha of forest from burning over
five years (indicative, pending damage data from the Directorate General of Forests, DGF).
Problem: More than 15,000 ha of Tunisian forest burned in summer 2026, five times the
2025 figure [10], and 95% of the fires had human causes [1]. The most vulnerable massifs (Sejnane,
Nefza, Tabarka, Ain Draham) lie in Kroumirie-Mogods [15]. Regional alerts exist, but the last
kilometre fails: the warning does not reach the right household in time. In Jendouba, 28.5% of
residents are illiterate, so a text alert misses many of them [27]. And in the same weeks the grid
failed: a night blackout from Greater Tunis to Tataouine on 14–15 July, then daily rolling cuts
while more than 900 fires broke out in 72 hours [3, 4]. A warning that depends on mains power
fails exactly when it is needed.
Solution: Solar sensor nodes at trails, roads and picnic areas hear an ignition with a
microphone and an on-device classifier. They confirm it with an infrared flame sensor (primary)
and an MQ-5 gas sensor (corroborating), and relay it over a licence-free 868 MHz LoRa mesh.
A backend grades each event as high, medium or low confidence; only confirmed events reach
residents. An escalation ladder starts at T+0 with a local siren, a voice call answered by pressing
1, and a Civil Protection alert. It adds a community guard at T+5 min and mosque or municipal
loudspeakers at T+10 min.
Status:
• Electronics: Interfaces checked in LTspice; the KiCad schematic passes the electrical rules
check.
• Energy: We found and fixed a critical flaw: the gas sensor alone used 3,600 mAh a day.
Duty-cycled, the node draws 53.6 mA in the nominal case, which a 2 W panel covers, and
about 68 mA with converter losses, which needs a 1-in-10 heater cycle or a 3 W panel. Bench
tests decide.
• Radio: Settings are configured to Tunisian ANF limits (25 mW, under 1% airtime).
• Software: The full decision chain runs in a Node-RED/MQTT simulation.
• Mechanical and partner: Enclosure modelled in SolidWorks; a telecom infrastructure
company is discussing hosting 2–3 gateways on its towers.
• Community testimony: A resident of Cap Negro whose family home was destroyed by a
fire in July 2026 reported that emergency response crews were unable to reach the site by
road (Section 1.3.4).
Market and impact: In mid-2026 the DGF announced start-up and AI programmes for early
detection [15, 16]. Existing offers detect fires, but none we found closes the last mile for residents
who cannot read. Qabs sells to the DGF and municipalities as equipment plus maintenance, or as
a service. A node costs about 317 TND today and about 200 TND on a custom board. Qabs
advances SDGs 13, 15, 11, 3 and 9.
1.2 Mission, Vision and Values
Mission: Detect forest fires in the Kroumirie-Mogods region at their earliest stages and deliver
timely, actionable alerts to those capable of responding, enabling intervention within minutes.
Vision: By 2030, every community living near Tunisia’s forests will receive timely fire alerts,
ensuring that no one learns of a wildfire only when smoke appears on the horizon.

1.3.1
Fire season, blackout season
The fires came with the darkness. On the night of 14 to 15 July 2026, at about 23:00, the
lights went out across areas from Greater Tunis to Tataouine while the night stayed above 32 ◦ C.
Firefighters were called to free people trapped in lifts, and residents who phoned the electricity
utility, STEG, got no answer [4]. Two days later STEG announced intermittent cuts in some 87
localities, including Bou Salem and Ghardimaou in Jendouba governorate, with power returning
“without notice” [5, 6]. By 20–22 July, when more than 900 fires broke out in 72 hours and
temperatures reached 40–49 ◦ C, planned cuts had become a daily routine [3]. Peak demand
reportedly approached 6,000 MW, about the country’s entire installed capacity (5,983 MW at
the end of 2024) [7], and STEG’s chief executive warned that restoring the grid after a general
blackout could take more than 24 hours [8].
2Backup power is no guarantee: on one farm a generator overheated during a long cut and
about 90% of the poultry died [9].
What this means for a warning. A siren on mains power, a router feeding an alert app, a
phone that cannot be recharged: each fails at the moment it is needed, in the very weeks when
fires peak. Qabs therefore assumes the grid is absent. Every node, gateway and siren runs on
its own panel and battery, and the first warning, the village siren, needs neither the grid nor the
internet (Table 4).
1.3.2
Why Kroumirie-Mogods
In June 2026 the Director General of Forests named Sejnane, Nefza, Tabarka and Ain Draham, all
in this corridor, as Tunisia’s most vulnerable massifs [15]. The territory covers about 2,900 km2 .
It holds about 48% of Tunisia’s cork oak, which has shrunk from 127,000 ha in 1952 to about
63,000 ha [24]. It is also home to about 62% of the country’s forest communities, who live from
cork, honey and aromatic plants [25]. Inside the corridor, 2026 fires struck Fernana and Balta-Bou
Aouane in July, damaging homes and killing livestock [17], the Kharkalia forest at Cap Negro [21],
a series of simultaneous fires around Nefza in August [22], and a lightning fire near Tabarka
in April [12]. No public data gives today’s detection delay in the corridor; Phase 2 asks Civil
Protection for alert logs to set that baseline. Jendouba also has Tunisia’s highest illiteracy rate:
28.5%, and 36.5% among women, against 17.3% nationally [27].
1.3.3
Fires start where people are, and the warning stops short
The forest administration names camping, cooking, farm machinery, power-line sparks, stubble
burning, cigarettes and arson as the main causes [16]. Many ignitions happen at night: in July
2021, 18 forest fires started between 20:00 and 05:00, and natural causes were at most 4% [18].
Ignition points are therefore predictable: trails, forest roads, picnic areas and field edges. A sensor
there catches a fire at the scale of one burning bush, and sound and infrared work in darkness.
Speed decides the outcome. In 2021, Civil Protection reached 60% of fires in under 15 minutes,
and 72% of fires were contained thanks to rapid response [19]. Yet field reports are still collected
on paper and spreadsheets [20], and public warnings are regional and text- or media-based. They
do not tell a shepherd near Ain Draham that a fire started 800 m up the trail five minutes ago.
This is the challenge’s own problem statement: losses come from data that does not reach a
household in time to act.
1.3.4
A voice from Cap Negro: when trucks cannot reach the fire
In July 2026 fire swept through the Kharkalia forest at Cap Negro, in the Nefza area of Béja
governorate, inside the corridor [21]. We spoke with a young woman whose family home there
burned that summer. Hers is one account from one conversation, not yet a structured interview,
but it names a gap that detection alone does not close:
• No road to the fire. She told us the areas that burned cannot be reached by vehicle,
so Civil Protection trucks could not get close. Reporting on the Nefza fires a month later
describes the same constraint: rugged terrain slows the tanker trucks [22].
• No other way in, in time. In her experience no helicopter or other specialised equipment
arrived in time, and the fire grew while crews looked for a way to reach it.
What this changes for Qabs. Where trucks cannot follow, the minutes gained at ignition
are worth the most, and the first warning must reach the people already there. Qabs therefore:
• stores the nearest track and access point for every node, so the first alert tells crews and
guards how to get there, not only where the fire is;
3• flags hard-to-reach nodes on the dashboard, so Civil Protection can request aerial or heavy
means at the first alert instead of after a failed ground approach;
• warns residents and the community guard at T+0, so households can protect themselves
and leave early when help may be delayed;
• gives priority, when siting nodes, to ignition points far from any road, where a late detection
costs the most.
What residents need. Together, the fire data, the census and this testimony define the
need Qabs must meet: a warning understood without reading, that works when the grid and the
network fail, that tells responders exactly where the fire is and how to reach it, and that is rarely
wrong, so people keep trusting it.
1.4 Solution
Qabs is a complete chain, not just a sensor. It hears an ignition at the trail, confirms it, carries it
over its own radio network, grades it, and escalates it until a person acknowledges. The five stages
map directly onto the challenge’s Detects → Communicates → Coordinates → Enables Response.
Figure 1: Qabs pipeline: five stages from ignition to people
What sets Qabs apart: it watches where fires start, confirms before it alarms, escalates until
someone acts, keeps its sirens and LoRa receivers sounding through power and network outages,
and is built and repaired in Tunisia. Figure 11 shows the sensor node that starts this chain.
2 System Function and Features
2.1 System Architecture
Qabs has four layers: field devices at the trail, a LoRa network, a backend that decides and
escalates, and the people who act (Figure 2). Sirens keep working even when the backend or the
internet fails.
The sensor node runs detection and confirmation itself. The siren unit and household receivers
listen on LoRa, so they sound without the internet. Household receivers work only inside mesh
range, a limit we state rather than hide.
4Figure 2: System architecture: four layers, thirteen components
What is new in this architecture.
• Listening at the ignition point: sound and an on-device classifier catch a fire at the
scale of one burning bush, day or night, where cameras and satellites see it only once it is
visible from afar.
• Graded confirmation instead of an AND gate: no real fire is silently dropped, and
unconfirmed events never reach residents.
• A first warning that needs no internet: sirens are triggered directly over LoRa, so the
warning survives grid and network failures.
• An alert that climbs until a person acts: siren and voice call first, then guards and
loudspeakers, so people who cannot read or have no phone are reached too.
2.2 Detection and Graded-Confidence Decision Logic
The microphone only nominates a candidate; confirmation decides what happens next. The IR
flame sensor is the primary confirmation, and the gas sensor can raise confidence but never block
an alert (Figure 3).
Why graded, not an AND gate. Our first design required the microphone and the gas
sensor to agree. Reviewing it, we found a silent failure: outdoors, wind disperses combustion
gases, so the MQ-5 often reads nothing and a real fire would have been dropped without a trace.
Now every candidate is kept, and the strength of confirmation decides who is told.

How confirmation works.
• IR flicker. Flames flicker at roughly 1–15 Hz, but so can sunlight through moving leaves.
The IR sensor therefore looks through a narrow shroud at the spot it watches, never at open
sky. Cheap IR modules see a lighter flame at about 1 m and saturate in daylight. Range
grows with flame size: a 1 m2 fire radiates thousands of times more than a lighter, so tens
of metres are plausible in shade, but this is unproven. Phase 2 measures range in sun and
shade and evaluates a filtered 4.3 μm pyroelectric flame sensor.
• Gas against a baseline. The heater runs for 60 s every 6 minutes, and gas is read only at
the end of each period, once the element is warm, against readings taken at the same point
of earlier periods. The datasheet’s 24-hour preheat sets absolute accuracy, which a relative
baseline does not need; Phase 2 checks that 60 s is enough outdoors. A candidate between
periods starts one at once, and a gas confirmation about a minute later upgrades low to
medium.
• Persistence (design rule). Crackle repeating on one node for two minutes is upgraded to
medium even without IR or gas. If IR proves shorter than 5 m outdoors, most real fires
take this path: Civil Protection is alerted about two minutes after the first crackle instead
of 15 s, and residents once a guard or Civil Protection confirms on the dashboard.
• Scope. Qabs detects the flaming stage. A cigarette smouldering in litter makes no crackle;
Phase 2 tests a low-power VOC gas sensor (for example the Bosch BME688) as a smoke
channel for that phase.
• Site profiles. At designated barbecue spots, alerts go first to the forest guard and Civil
Protection. Residents are alerted if the event persists or spreads.

2.3 Community Escalation Ladder and Inclusion by Design
Every confirmed alert climbs a three-step ladder, one step every 5 minutes, until someone handles
it (Figure 4). Voice comes before text, and the last step reaches people who have no phone.
Why this order. The siren needs no network and also reaches visitors on the trail. Voice
replaces SMS because 28.5% of Jendouba residents, and 36.5% of women, are illiterate. Calls play
a short recorded message in Tunisian Arabic: where the fire is, which way to move, and “press 1
if you heard this”. Civil Protection is alerted at T+0, never after residents. A community guard
checks on foot at T+5, catching phones that are off and helping elderly neighbours. Mosque and
municipal loudspeakers at T+10 reach homes with no phone.
Figure 4: Community escalation ladder: three steps, five minutes apart
Who is called. Municipalities build an opt-in call list at community meetings, linking
households to the nodes that cover them, plus one guard per hamlet. Pressing 1 only stops further
calls to that household. The ladder climbs to T+5 and T+10 unless Civil Protection or the guard
has marked the event as handled.
Honest limits. Household receivers work only within mesh range; voice calls need mobile
coverage at the home; and a siren helps only if people hear it and know what it means. A 110 dB
siren (at 1 m) falls to about 70 dB at 100 m and 56 dB at 500 m in open air, less behind trees
and walls, so each siren stands at the centre of one hamlet: the pilot’s three sirens serve three
hamlets. Each pilot also includes a drill and one instruction: leave the forest and go to the agreed
meeting point.

3 Powering the Node: Energy Budget
In the nominal case the node averages 53.6 mA, or 1,286 mAh a day, and a 2 W panel covers it
with about +114 mAh a day to spare. Counting the losses of the 5 V heater rail, the conservative
case is about 68 mA and the margin turns negative, so the design keeps two fallbacks: a 1-in-10
heater cycle or a 3 W panel. Both cases rest on a base current that Phase 2 must measure
(Table 5).
3.1 System Power Requirements
Gas sensors of the MQ family only react when their sensing element is warm, so the MQ-5 carries
a tiny built-in heater: a coil of about 31 Ω inside a ceramic tube, using under 0.8 W [37]. It is
sealed under the sensor’s own stainless-steel flame-arrestor gauze, designed not to ignite even the
flammable gas it measures, so it is not a fire risk (Section 5.3). Its real cost is energy: it draws
about 150 mA. Left on continuously, it alone used 3,600 mAh a day and the node 4,287 mAh,
more than any panel that fits a 150 mm box could supply. The fix: power the heater only about
one-sixth of the time, on periodic wake-ups. The rest of the node draws a target of 28.6 mA
(Table 5), so the average becomes 28.6 + 150/6 ≈ 53.6 mA, with the heater current as specified
(see the open check in Section 3.3).

3.2 Solar Power System
North-West Tunisia yields about 1,450 kWh per kWp a year, or 3.97 kWh per kWp a day [42].
With about 65% end-to-end efficiency for a linear charger, the 2 W panel delivers 2 W × 3.97 h ×
0.65 ≈ 5.2 Wh a day, or about 1,400 mAh at 3.7 V. This is consistent with the +114 mAh margin
(about +108 mAh with unrounded figures).
3.3 Seasons, Autonomy and Open Checks
• Seasons: Margins are largest from May to October, when fire risk peaks. In January
northern irradiance falls to about 1.9 kWh/m2 a day [43], below the 686 mAh a day the
node needs even with the gas sensor off. Winter mode therefore also duty-cycles the audio
pipeline and lengthens heartbeats, and panels are tilted at about 50◦ for the low winter sun.
• Shade: Under canopy a panel gets only part of the open-sky sun. Nodes are sited at
trailheads and clearings with the panel on the pole top, and Phase 2 sizes each site from
monthly PVGIS data with a shading factor; shaded sites get a 3 W panel.
• Autonomy: One 3,000 mAh cell lasts about 1.9 days without sun at 80% depth of discharge.
Two cells give about 3.7 days in the nominal case but 2.9 days at 68 mA, so the conservative
case needs a third cell (about 10 TND each) to keep the 3-day requirement.
• Heater rail losses: The MQ-5 heater is specified at 5 V, so its 150 mA is drawn on the
5 V rail. Referred to the 3.7 V cell through a boost converter, it becomes about 240 mA,
9raising the node average to about 68 mA and turning the margin negative. The fallback is a
1-in-10 heater duty cycle (≈52 mA, so the 2 W panel still closes) or a 3 W panel. Phase 2
bench tests will settle this and measure the base current of Table 5.

4.2 Part Choices
Audio is sampled at 16 kHz, which captures sound up to 8 kHz and so covers the 1–3 kHz
band where fire crackle concentrates; surface-fire sound extends to about 15 kHz [30, 31]. The
MAX9814’s automatic gain control keeps faint and loud events in range. The IR sensor responds
in milliseconds and, unlike gas, does not depend on wind. The MQ-5 is cheap but has low smoke
sensitivity and needs a warm-up [37]. Phase 2 therefore compares it with MQ-2, MQ-7 and
low-power digital gas sensors that draw a few milliamps instead of 150 mA, and an I2S MEMS
microphone that could replace the MAX9814 with lower power and cleaner audio.

5.3 Mechanical Design
The enclosure is a printed box of about 150 × 110 × 70 mm, with an IP65-class sealed electronics
bay and three different sensor openings, because each sensor needs outside air or light in a different
way.
Enclosure. It is printed in ASA or PETG, never PLA, which softens at about 55–60 ◦ C inside
a sun-heated box; ASA also resists UV. Inside there are about 90 × 70 × 35 mm of usable space,
and the 18650 cell is the tallest part. A gasket in a lid groove seals the electronics bay, with a
drain at the lowest point as mounted. Open ports cannot be dust-tight, so Phase 2 covers the
acoustic port, gas vent and drain with ePTFE (expanded PTFE) vent membranes, the breathable
film of waterproof jackets: its pores are about 20,000 times smaller than a water drop but 700
times larger than a water-vapour molecule [39]. Rain and dust stay out while gas and sound
pass, so the MQ-5 still smells the smoke and the microphone still hears the crackle; Phase 2
measures the loss in the 1–3 kHz band. The IR opening gets a polycarbonate window, which
passes near-infrared. The design starts from RAK Wireless’s parametric enclosure, modified with
our ports (Figure 11). It is strapped to a pole or tree at about 2.5 m, out of casual reach, in a
neutral colour that discourages vandalism.

Can the node itself start a fire? A heater inside a forest sensor deserves this question.
The answer is no, by design:
• The heater is tiny and enclosed. It uses under 0.8 W, less than a small LED bulb, and
sits inside a ceramic tube under a stainless-steel 100-mesh “anti-explosion” gauze [37]. The
MQ-5 is built to sit in leaking cooking gas and detect it without igniting it.
• Its outside stays far from ignition temperatures. Dried leaves need a surface of at
least 300 ◦ C for ten minutes, or 500 ◦ C for a few seconds [38]; the sensor sits on a plastic
base on an ordinary circuit board, which could not survive anything close to that.
• It is sealed away and rarely on. It has its own compartment in the closed box and never
touches vegetation; it is powered about one-sixth of the time, and its switch is off at reset,
so it stays off whenever the board restarts.
• The battery is guarded too. The Li-ion cell holds most of the stored energy: the charger’s
protection cuts it off on a short circuit or deep discharge, it charges only between 0 and
45 ◦ C, and it sits at the shaded base.

6 Communication, Edge AI and Software
6.1 Communications Design
Nodes talk over licence-free LoRa at 868 MHz, capped at 25 mW and under 1% airtime, the
European short-range-device limits that Tunisia applies (Section 6.2). Gateways sit high on
telecom towers to overcome forest attenuation.
• Mesh to gateways: Nodes relay each other’s events (store-and-forward, limited hops,
duplicate suppression) to 2–3 solar gateways on towers or ridges. Gateways forward events
over 4G with 2G fallback; 99.9% of the population is covered by 2G/3G/4G [28], but
mountain hamlets may not be, so the walk test checks each gateway’s link on site.
• Packets: An event packet of about 40 bytes, including authentication, carries node ID,
time, classifier score, IR flicker score, gas reading and battery voltage. Periodic heartbeats
reveal a silent node within hours.
• Local sirens: Sirens are triggered over LoRa by a gateway or a nearby node, so the first
warning needs no internet.
• Security: Payloads use authenticated encryption (AES-CCM) with message counters, so
fake or replayed alarms are rejected. ESP32 flash encryption and secure boot protect keys if
a node is stolen.
• Power of the other devices: gateway about 60–100 mA (2 Ah a day) and siren unit
about 30–40 mA while listening, each on a 10 W panel with three days of battery; household
receivers listen in short windows (about 2 mA) on a USB charger with an 18650 backup
that lasts weeks in a blackout.
6.2 Compliance with Tunisian ANF Rules
Tunisia applies the European short-range-device limits (CEPT ERC/REC 70-03, tested to ETSI
EN 300 220 [44, 45]; exact ANF reference to confirm). In 868.0–868.6 MHz, devices are licence-free
at up to 25 mW ERP (14 dBm) and under 1% airtime.
• Power: Output is capped at +14 dBm in firmware, never the radio’s +20 dBm. Many
LoRa32 boards wire only the SX1276’s PA BOOST output to the antenna, so its register
15is limited to +14 dBm. With the stock antenna of about 2 dBi this gives about 13.9 dBm
ERP; if measured margin is thin, +13 dBm.
• Airtime: 1% means 36 s per hour. A 40-byte packet takes about 0.29 s at spreading factor
9 (about 125 packets an hour) and 2.0 s at spreading factor 12 (about 18); SF12 is kept
for the few longest links. Relaying counts against each node’s own 1%: forwarding hourly
20-byte heartbeats (about 0.19 s each) for ten neighbours plus its own takes about 2 s an
hour, and relaying one fire event three times with retries about 2 s more. A pure-ALOHA
estimate gives about 0.2% collisions for heartbeats. A fire is the hard case: nine 0.29 s
packets from three nodes and their relays within 10 s would collide about 40% of the time if
sent freely, so each node sends in a slot set by its ID and only one relay forwards each event.
Phase 2 tests this.

Measured 868 MHz links reach about 860 m in dense forest and 2,050 m in less dense forest [34].
Raising the receiver to 25 m extended range to about 1.75 km [35]. Forest adds about 25 dB of

6.4 Edge AI Classifier and Data Strategy
A small neural network on the ESP32 decides, second by second, whether the microphone hears
fire. It is trained first on public datasets, then on recordings from Tunisian forests. Raw audio
never leaves the node.
On the node: Audio at 16 kHz is cut into 1-second windows with 50% overlap. Burning
vegetation produces bursts of short acoustic impulses [32], so a cheap impulse counter passes only
crackle-like windows to the network. A compact 8-bit convolutional network then scores each
window’s log-mel spectrogram, using TensorFlow Lite Micro or ESP-DL; the targets are under
100 kB and 100 ms. A candidate needs several consecutive windows to agree, for example 3 of 5.
Training data: ESC-50 and FSD50K provide fire and crackle clips. FSC22 adds 2,025
forest clips in 27 classes, including fire, rain, wind, insects, chainsaws and vehicles [33]. Local
hard negatives cover summer cicadas, wind in cork oaks, livestock bells, motorbikes and cooking.
Phase 2 starts with small supervised bench burns; controlled burns of local fuels (cork oak litter,
heather, lentisk, pine), recorded at 5–50 m with forest and Civil Protection staff, follow in spring
2027 and give the detection-distance curve that sets node spacing. The 50 m target comes from
siting, not acoustics: it assumes fires at a trailhead start within about 50 m of where people stop,
and the burns will show whether the microphone reaches that far.
Validation and governance:
• Splits: Field data is split by site, so the model is always tested on a place it has never
heard.
16• Reporting: Confusion matrix, false candidates per node per day, and detection probability
against distance.
• Target: At least 95% recall at the candidate stage; confirmation removes false positives.
• Updates: Models ship only in validated firmware updates.
• Privacy: Deployed nodes never store or transmit audio. Training recordings are made only
in organised sessions with consent, and human speech is discarded.

8 Market Analysis and Business Model
8.1 Market and Customers
Demand is real and funded now: in June and July 2026 the DGF announced start-up partnerships
and a push toward AI for early warning [15, 16]. Existing offers detect fires; none we found closes
the last mile.
Customers and channels: The DGF and its regional districts (Jendouba, Béja, Bizerte)
are the primary buyer. Corridor municipalities, which run loudspeakers and can host guards,
are secondary. Civil Protection, forest guards and residents use Qabs without paying. Funding
channels include the EU, which backed Tunisia’s July 2026 firefighting through its Civil Protection
Mechanism [14], the Interreg FORFIRE-AI programme [50] and WWF North Africa’s digital
fire-monitoring tender for the DGF [20].
Market size: planning estimates, assuming 5% of forest is high-risk human interface with
one node per 10 ha of it; the DGF can replace them with its own data.

the cost of protecting each hectare, sevenfold. The projection in Section 8.6 equips the whole

9.4 Sustainability and Lifecycle Resilience
Lifecycle: Parts are sourced and enclosures printed in Tunisia. Nodes sense fully from May to
October and run a low-power mode in winter. Each April one visit cleans the IR window and
foam, checks the gasket, tests the siren and updates firmware over Wi-Fi; batteries last 2–3 years,
then cells are recycled and enclosures reprinted. Maintenance fees fund replacements; the DGF
and municipalities own the system.
2510 General Conclusion
Tunisia does not lack fire data; it lacks a way to turn the first minutes of a fire into action by the
people closest to it. The summer of 2026 made that gap visible: more than 15,000 ha of forest
burned, more than 900 fires broke out in 72 hours, the grid failed in the same weeks, and a Cap
Negro resident told us crews could not reach the fire by road. Qabs answers with low-cost sensing
at ignition points and an escalation ladder that reaches residents who cannot read or have no
phone, and its sirens keep sounding when the power and the network do not.
What Phase 1 established.
• A complete chain, not just a sensor: detection at the trail, graded confirmation, a
LoRa network of its own, a backend that escalates, and a ladder that ends only when a
person acknowledges the alert. The full decision chain already runs in a Node-RED/MQTT
simulation.
• Numbers that can be checked: a design target of about 15 s from crackle to Civil
Protection, an average draw of 53.6 mA (68 mA conservative) against about 1,400 mAh a
day from a 2 W panel, radio settings within the ANF limits, and a node at about 317 TND
today and 200 TND on a custom board.
• Three flaws caught on paper: gas-sensor energy use, radio power above the legal limit,
and an AND gate that would have silently dropped real fires. All three were found and fixed
before any hardware was built.
• A design shaped by people: the Cap Negro testimony added an access point and a
hard-to-reach flag to every alert, the illiteracy figures put sirens and voice before text, and
privacy, equity and human decision are written in as design rules.
Phase 2 demonstration plan (10 December 2026), ordered by the risk each step retires:
1. Bench evidence: node current including the heater rail, IR range in sun and shade, flicker
rejection, and classifier results with false alarms per hour.
2. A recorded crackle and a small supervised flame trigger the full chain within one minute:
alert, siren, voice call answered with 1, dashboard.
3. A forest LoRa walk test; then three nodes, a solar gateway and a siren unit, with a fault
demo (backhaul unplugged, the siren still sounds).
After Phase 2. A 30-node pilot near Ain Draham, installed in April 2027 for a full fire
season, will be judged against clear gates: at least 90% of blind test fires detected and reported
within 5 minutes, at most one false siren per site, and node uptime of at least 95%. If it passes,
the corridor follows in 2028, owned by the DGF and municipalities and run by local technicians
and guards.
Every forest fire starts as a single spark. Our aim is that the people around it know within
seconds, not when the smoke reaches the horizon.
